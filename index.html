The first line for routine GLP-1 questions, so the practice isn't.

People with type 2 diabetes starting a GLP-1 get the pen and a leaflet. Then they ring the practice about nausea, a missed dose, whether they can have a drink. First Line answers the routine ones from clinician-approved content, and sends anything symptom-shaped straight to a person.

How it works

Three taps. Two outcomes. The judgement is always a person's.

What's in it

Six things, each deliberately small.

Ask, from an approved library

Eight-plus clinician-approved answers in v1, each with its source and version. Update the library once; every patient sees the change.

“Keep unused pens in the fridge. Once in use, room temperature for up to 21 days.”
Patient information leaflet, v3

The gate

Anything symptom-shaped gets the same card for everyone: here's the red-flag list, here's a person. No interpretation, no urgency score, no exceptions.

“That sounds like something a person should look at, not an app.”

Diary: a record, not a score

Each weekly dose logged with an optional note. Stored and shown. No adherence percentage, no streak, no nudges. A person reads it.

Guide: the leaflet, made readable

The standard titration schedule, the manufacturer's missed-dose rules and common early effects. Identical for every patient. The patient applies it; the app never adjusts it.

Help: red flags and a real person

A generic safety-netting list with 111 and 999 one tap away, and live chat that goes to a pharmacist. The app carries the message; the pharmacist decides.

Guided safety check (locked)

Three yes/no questions that route straight to the right help. Because it makes a judgement from patient answers, it's a Class IIa device. Shown in the app, built for v2 on v1 evidence.

The clinician view, in the patient's words

For the practice pharmacist or GP. Every question a patient asked, tagged answered from library, routed to a person or not in library. Their diary notes verbatim. The live chat, with a human reply box. No summary, no risk score. The software surfaces, a person decides.

Regulation, honestly

Class I today. Deliberately.

Under UK MDR Rule 11, software becomes a medical device needing Approved Body assessment when it acts on patient data beyond storage, display or communication to inform an individual's diagnosis or treatment. Every component of v1 is scoped so that none does.

That means UKCA self-declaration, a hazard log from day one, and a product a practice could put in front of patients now, while v1 usage generates the evidence a Class IIa version would need.

DiaryStorage and display of what the patient entered. No adherence interpretation.
GuideStatic reference content, identical for every user.
HelpGeneric safety-netting list, equivalent to the printed leaflet.
Live chatA human-delivered service. Software routes and transports the message.
AskAnswers only from a fixed, approved library. Rejects symptom-shaped input.
Safety checkClass IIa · v2  Stratifies urgency from patient answers. Built, locked, not shipped.
